Direction is where the storm comes from, speed in knots. Guessing it off one frame is near impossible, so use the Motion tool on the map: mark a feature, step forward a few scans, click it again. Hovering a warning polygon shows the motion the NWS put in it, and shift-clicking the polygon adopts it.

Detail is the width of the picture the sweep is drawn into; Range is how much of the sweep it covers. They trade against each other: the same pixels over a smaller square is more detail on the storm and none at all past the edge. The square is centred on the radar site, not on you — crop to 120 km and a cell 200 km from the radar is outside the picture, however close it is to your car.

Color Tables

GR2Analyst and GRLevel3 color tables — the same .pal files you already use. Drop several in at once and each one lands on the product its Product: line names; the per-product Load buttons override that. Color, Color4, SolidColor, SolidColor4, RF, Scale, Offset and comments are all read, including the two-color form that puts a hard edge at a threshold.

A loaded table sets its own value range, so the bar reads the way it does in GR. Velocity tables that say Units: KTS without a Scale are read as knots — the row says which was assumed, so check it if a table looks half or double what it should.

Live

Live updates off.

Completed volumes polls for finished scans, so you sit one scan behind — about 4–6 minutes in precip mode, longer in clear air. Real-time reads the chunk feed instead and paints the sweep while the antenna is still turning, seconds behind, so the newest part of the scan is drawn and the rest of the circle stays empty until it comes round.

Raw Archive II straight from NOAA’s AWS bucket, decoded in this page. A volume climbs to 19.5° in fourteen cuts and the upper ones are most of the file, so the default stops at 5° — eight cuts, every tilt you would arrow through — and never downloads the rest. Raise it if you want echo tops to see the top of a storm close to the radar. Either way the picture appears as soon as the tilt you are looking at is ready — about half a second — rather than waiting for the whole scan. Load it all then reads every scan in the window to the ceiling, keeps them, and draws every tilt in the background — a minute or so for an hour of data, after which every frame and every tilt is instant, with nothing decoded twice. Expect a few hundred megabytes held for an hour’s window; the panel above says what fits. Lean decodes and draws only the tilt you are looking at and holds a fraction of that; pick it on a machine short of memory. Every product works in both, dual-pol included — Lean simply reads one sweep at a time instead of the whole window. Scan times are the real ones (about 4–6 min apart), not a fixed grid.

Polygons come from the NWS storm-based-warning archive and follow the timeline, so an archived chase shows exactly what was out at that minute — including the mid-warning polygon updates. Hover the outline for the tags; there is no fill, so the echo underneath stays visible and the middle of a polygon is not a hover trap. A dashed outline is radar-indicated; solid is observed; PDS and tornado emergencies are called out.

Every storm-based warning the archive holds is drawn, including codes this app has no color for — those come through grey, named from the feed. If a count above says warnings are hidden, it is these boxes doing it.

SPC Storm Reports

Storm report overlay off.

Reports come from the Storm Prediction Center’s daily tornado, hail and wind files and nowhere else. Each icon appears at the report’s time and stays for one hour of radar feed, fading as it ages, then goes. Hail is labelled in inches, wind in mph; two-inch hail, 75 mph wind and EF2+ are flagged significant. Hover one for the location and the spotter’s remark.

SPC publishes by convective day — 12Z to 12Z — so an overnight chase pulls the previous date’s file. That is handled for you.

Live and archived both work. A finished day is read once and never again — it cannot change. The day in progress is a file SPC is still appending to, so it is re-read every few minutes for as long as the timeline reaches into it, whether the radar is the live loop, a Level II feed or an archive window that runs up to now. Reports reach SPC a few minutes after the event, so on a live chase an icon appears a scan or two after the thing it describes.

If your browser refuses to read spc.noaa.gov from a local file, download YYMMDD_rpts_torn.csv, _hail and _wind from spc.noaa.gov/climo/reports and load them with the button above.

Appearance & Basemap

Dark is the default and the easiest to read — the panels are frosted enough to win against any storm behind them. Light is the same thing inverted, for daylight. Clear is thin dark glass: the cell under the sidebar shows through and the text carries its own shadow to stay legible — lovely to look at, harder to read over a bright core.

Turn the blur off for flat solid panels. Worth doing on a slow laptop: a panel this size costs the graphics card a pass every frame the map moves, and the app checks for that on startup anyway.

The dark and light basemaps come from CARTO, which watermarks its raster tiles unless the request carries an API key. One is built in; paste your own here if it is ever rotated, or clear the field to go without. Changing it redraws the tiles straight away. The other styles — satellite, streets, topographic, OSM — do not use it.

Each track carries the storm motion that was set when you dropped it, and is carried along by it as you step scans — so on a later frame the marker either lands on the cell or it does not, which tells you whether the vector was any good. Ticks are 15, 30, 45 and 60 minutes ahead.

Radar scans are fixed at 5 minutes and can't be subdivided. A shorter vehicle clock steps your car between scans, so you watch it drive while the radar image is held — the same lag you get in the field. The clock can also run on past the newest scan, so you're never waiting on radar to move the car.

Reach shades every road by how long it takes to drive there from where the car is right now. Roads, not a smooth blob — you drive roads, and a filled-in isochrone claims you can be in the middle of a field.

Escape asks a harder question: is there a road to this place that stays out of the core the whole way, and will you still have time when you get there? Yours — yes, with margin to spare. Tight — yes, but only just. Stays clear — yes, and the core never comes near it. No clear way — every route there drives through the storm, or it beats you. In it now — the core is on that road at this moment.

The core is taken from the reflectivity on screen, at the threshold above, and carried forward with the storm’s motion: what will be on a road in ten minutes is what sits ten minutes upstream of it now. The search itself refuses to cross it — so a road is only green if the route is clear, not just the destination. An earlier cut of this checked only the destination, and happily sent you through the hook to reach a field beyond it.

It needs a storm track for the motion — drop one with the Track tool first. On the IEM composite there are no radar values to sample, only pictures, so it falls back to a moving circle and says so; that is a guess at the shape and should be treated as one.

Motion from radar stops the motion being a guess. Two consecutive scans of the same storm are nearly the same picture shifted a little way, so the shift that best lines them up is the motion. It searches for that shift and writes the answer into the selected track — or into the storm-motion boxes if no track is selected — along with how well the two scans matched. A poor match means the storm changed shape rather than simply moved, and it says so instead of inventing a number.

Speeds are the same ones a driven leg uses — the state DOT’s posted limits, then OSM, then road class, with the gravel and dirt caps. The one difference is that the router’s own per-segment speed cannot apply here, because there is no route yet to annotate.

Signals and stop signs are not counted. This is a picture of the road network, so it runs a few minutes optimistic in town; a driven leg costs them properly.

Speed

No legs driven yet.

Each segment takes the best number available for the road it is actually on, in this order:

1. The state DOT’s posted limit. The agency’s own record of what the sign says, which outranks a volunteered tag for the same road. Texas only so far, via TxDOT’s published speed-limit layer — and it is not just the state highways: numbered county roads are in there too, carrying real posted values where OSM out there has almost nothing.

2. The OSM posted limit, from that road’s maxspeed tag. Volunteered, but everywhere.

3. The router’s own speed for that exact segment. The routing engine has to work out how fast every piece of the route goes in order to give an arrival time at all, so this comes back with the route itself — no extra request, and it is there even when the OSM lookup fails completely. It is built from maxspeed where the road carries one and the road’s class where it does not. This is why a chase can no longer come out flat.

4. The road class from the tick box above: interstate 70, US highway 60, state highway 55, county road 45, residential 28. A coarse table, used only where the router said nothing.

5. The fallback limit above, one flat number, genuinely last.

The leg breakdown always says which of the five set each speed, so an estimate is never passed off as a posted sign. The under-the-limit buffer applies only to a posted sign — a router speed is already a travel speed, so taking it off again would be charging the same discount twice.

The surface caps come after the limit, because they are a limit of the vehicle rather than of the law — a posted 55 on a dirt section line is still 55, and you still cannot drive it at 55.

A limit tagged without units is read as mph. OSM’s US guidance makes the suffix mandatory, so a bare number is a mapper leaving it off, not a metric limit — and reading 55 as km/h would put you at 34 mph on a 55 road. Those segments are counted separately in the leg breakdown.
